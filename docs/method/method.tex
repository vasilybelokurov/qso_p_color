\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A quasar has a spectroscopic redshift $z_0$; a companion a few arcseconds away
has broadband photometry and nothing else. We want to know how strongly the
companion's photometry supports the hypothesis that it is a quasar at
$z\simeq z_0$. This note sets out the single model implemented in
\texttt{qso\_pcolor}. It accepts any combination of $41$ bands from SDSS,
Legacy Surveys DR9 (including its forced unWISE $W1$, $W2$), AllWISE,
Pan-STARRS1, NSC, SkyMapper and VHS, including infrared-only input. Quasars
and the field population are each described by an extreme-deconvolution
mixture over native band luptitudes, conditioned on one measured reference
band; missing bands are marginalised exactly. Surface densities per unit
reference luptitude, built separately for each reference band, turn the two
densities into intensities for four hypotheses: a quasar at $z_0$, a quasar at
another redshift, the modelled field, and a broad ``unmodelled'' share of the
field that stops Gaussian tails from deciding. A narrow redshift window enters
as a multiplicative constant, which defines the window-free ranking statistic
$R$. On $50{,}746$ companions of DESI quasars that have spectra of their own,
Legacy $g,r,z,W1,W2$ photometry ranks a same-redshift quasar above a
wrong-redshift one with held-out AUC $0.828$, and separates quasars from
stars and galaxies with AUC $0.982$ and $0.961$; the same-redshift
probability is low by a factor that falls from $9.8$ at $3$--$5''$ to
$3.3$ at $20$--$30''$, the signature of quasar clustering that the scorer
deliberately omits. Quasars just outside a $\pm3000\kms$ window cannot be
distinguished from those inside it. The model replaces an earlier
Legacy-only model (Appendix~\ref{app:predecessor}): on identical inputs it
ranks redshift better ($0.828$ against $0.815$) and rejects stars slightly less
well ($0.982$ against $0.988$). Across all $127$ survey combinations, reserved
quasars are separated from reserved field sources with median AUC $0.996$.
\end{abstract}

\section*{The method in plain words}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The method in plain words}

An orientation before the formalism. Everything asserted here is developed, and
qualified, in the sections that follow.

\paragraph{The question, and what is in scope.} We have a quasar whose redshift
has been measured from a spectrum, and a neighbour a few arcseconds away for
which we have only photometry --- a brightness in each of a handful of filters.
We want to know whether that neighbour is also a quasar, at the same redshift.
The pipeline scores only companions far enough from the primary that the survey
photometry treats them as separate sources; closer pairs need image-level work
that is not attempted here (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Whatever photometry exists.} A companion may have been measured by
some of seven surveys and not others. The model takes every usable measurement
it is given, from any subset of the seven, and integrates over the rest. Filters
from different surveys are never treated as the same filter, and the same survey
north and south of the Legacy Surveys' dividing line is treated as two systems.

\paragraph{How we learn what quasars look like.} We take spectroscopically
confirmed quasars from DESI DR1 and SDSS DR16Q, draw an equal number in every
redshift interval of width $0.1$, and attach whatever the seven surveys measured
for each of them. A fifth of the sky is held back before anything is fitted.
For each of a set of overlapping redshift slices we fit the joint distribution
of the brightnesses in all bands, and blend neighbouring slices according to
where a given redshift falls. At scoring time the distribution is conditioned
on one measured band --- the reference --- so the question it answers is:
\emph{if this object is a quasar at redshift $z$, and is this bright in the
reference band, how likely are its brightnesses in the other bands?}

That sample reflects the DESI and SDSS target selections, which are themselves
built on these colours. It is not an unbiased sample of quasars in general.

\paragraph{Why measurement error is handled explicitly.} Every measurement
carries an uncertainty. Fitting the observed cloud would give a locus broader
than the true one and would make unusual colours look less unusual than they
are. So we fit the \emph{underlying} distribution, using each training object's
own errors, and add each candidate's own errors back when scoring it. A noisy
candidate is compared against an appropriately blurred locus.

\paragraph{What we use for the competing population.} Not stars alone. We take
every source detected by any of the seven surveys in two dozen small fields
spread over the sky, remove the known quasars, and model all of it the same way:
stars, faint galaxies, blends, whatever the surveys contain. A chance neighbour
is drawn from everything that is there.

\paragraph{How the two are compared.} Two distributions of brightness give the
relative evidence --- reported --- but not a probability, because that also
depends on how many objects of each kind exist at this brightness. So each is
multiplied by a surface density, per unit brightness in the reference band:
quasars per square degree per unit redshift, and field sources per square degree.
Both are counted only inside the footprint of the survey that supplies the
reference band, because a band can be missing either because the object is
outside the survey or because it is too faint, and only the second says
anything about the object. The result is an expected-count density for each of
four possibilities:

\begin{itemize}
\item a quasar at the primary's redshift;
\item a quasar at some \emph{other} redshift;
\item anything else in the imaging;
\item something neither model describes.
\end{itemize}

The second is not a refinement. A quasar far behind the primary looks nothing
like a star, so a quasar-versus-star comparison would count it as evidence for a
companion; for a pair search that is the main way of being wrong. The fourth
exists because both models are sums of Gaussians. Far from everything either
was fitted to, their ratio is decided by whichever Gaussian tail happens to be
wider, and an artefact would be called a certain quasar. A small, fitted share
of the field with a heavy-tailed distribution takes those objects instead.

\paragraph{Why the answer is a ranking rather than a verdict.} Colours locate a
quasar in redshift only loosely --- typically to $\pm0.4$ --- while a
$\pm2000\kms$ pair window at $z=1.8$ spans $0.037$. Only a small part of any
candidate's redshift probability can fall inside the window, so the probability
is small even for a strong candidate. We therefore report a statistic that the
width of the window does not affect, in the narrow limit, and convert it to a
probability only for a window the user declares. Candidates are ordered by that
statistic; spectroscopy decides.

\paragraph{What the test against the truth shows.} About $50{,}000$ objects lie
within $30''$ of a known quasar \emph{and} have a DESI spectrum of their own.
Scored from Legacy photometry alone, a genuine same-redshift partner outranks a
random wrong-redshift quasar $83\%$ of the time, and fewer than one star in a
thousand scores as high as a typical same-redshift quasar.
Quasars just outside the velocity window are indistinguishable from those just
inside. And the probability the scorer reports is too small --- by about three
times for wide pairs and ten times for the closest --- because it treats the
companion as a random nearby object, while real quasars cluster. The factor is
that clustering.

\section{The question, and the question that can be answered}
\label{sec:question}

The scientific target is physical quasar pairs. One member has a spectrum and
the other does not, and obtaining the second spectrum is expensive. A method
that ranks companions by how quasar-like their photometry is \emph{at the
primary's redshift} decides where telescope time goes.

\paragraph{A likelihood is not a probability.} $p(\uvec\mid Q,z_0)$ --- how
probable this photometry is \emph{if} the object is a quasar at $z_0$ --- says
nothing on its own about whether the object is a quasar. Turning it into a
probability requires knowing how many quasars and how many other objects exist
at this brightness. We compute and report both, and never let the first be read
as the second.

\paragraph{Two hypotheses are not enough.} A calculation that pits ``quasar at
$z_0$'' against ``star'' assigns a high probability to a genuine quasar at
$z=2.6$ beside a primary at $z=1.4$. For a pair search that is the dominant
contaminant, so the competing hypotheses must include field quasars.

\section{Hypotheses and intensities}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

Let $\uvec$ be the measured band luptitudes (\S\ref{sec:photometry}), $u_a$ the
one chosen as reference, and $\uvec_{\setminus a}$ the others. We consider

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$\Hsame$ & a quasar whose redshift matches $z_0$ under a declared window \\
$\Hfield$ & a quasar at any other redshift \\
$\Hbkg$ & anything else in the imaging, as described by the field model \\
$\Hout$ & a broad, fitted share of the field that neither model describes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\Hbkg$ is deliberately not ``star''. The population a chance projection draws
from is everything the imaging contains, and it needs no selection function,
because it is exactly the set of objects being counted (\S\ref{sec:background}).

We work with \emph{intensities}: expected counts per square degree, per unit
reference luptitude, per unit volume of the other luptitudes. Writing
$W(z\mid z_0)\in[0,1]$ for the redshift-match weight, $\Sq(z,u_a)$ and
$\Sb(u_a)$ for the surface densities of objects measured in band $a$
(\S\ref{sec:priors}), and $\eta(u_a)$ for the unmodelled share of the field
(\S\ref{sec:outliers}),
%
\begin{align}
\lambda_{\rm same} &= \int W(z\mid z_0)\,\Sq(z,u_a)\,
                        p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z)\,\dd z,
\label{eq:lam_same}\\
\lambda_{\rm field} &= \int \bigl[1-W(z\mid z_0)\bigr]\,\Sq(z,u_a)\,
                        p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z)\,\dd z,
\label{eq:lam_field}\\
\lambda_{\rm bkg} &= [1-\eta(u_a)]\,\Sb(u_a)\,p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,B),
\label{eq:lam_bkg}\\
\lambda_{\rm out} &= \eta(u_a)\,\Sb(u_a)\,p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,U).
\label{eq:lam_out}
\end{align}
%
All four carry the same units, so their ratios are meaningful, and
%
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm same} =
\frac{\lambda_{\rm same}}
     {\lambda_{\rm same}+\lambda_{\rm field}+\lambda_{\rm bkg}+\lambda_{\rm out}} .
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
%
Alongside this we always report the colour Bayes factor
%
\begin{equation}
\ln \mathrm{BF} = \ln p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z_0)
  - \ln p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,\text{field}),
\qquad
p(\cdot\mid\text{field}) = (1-\eta)\,p(\cdot\mid B) + \eta\,p(\cdot\mid U),
\label{eq:bf}
\end{equation}
%
which depends on the density models and on the field's composition $\eta$ but
on neither surface density. If the surface densities change,
\eqref{eq:posterior} must move and \eqref{eq:bf} must not; a unit test enforces
it.

\section{Photometry}
\label{sec:photometry}

\subsection{Forty-one bands, each in its own system}
\label{sec:bands}

The model stores $41$ individually labelled bands: SDSS $ugriz$; Legacy
Surveys DR9 $g,r,z,W1,W2$, separately for the southern (DECam, releases
$9010$ and $9012$) and northern ($9011$) systems; AllWISE $W1$--$W4$; PS1
$grizy$; NSC $ugrizy$ and VR; SkyMapper $uvgriz$; and VHS $YJHK_s$. Filters
from different surveys are never identified with one another. In particular
the Legacy $W1,W2$ --- unWISE fluxes forced at the optical position and
deblended with the optical model --- and the AllWISE catalogue $W1,W2$, a
separate detection with a $\sim6''$ beam, are different measurements and are
separate bands. The distinction matters: the Legacy forced $W1,W2$ are the
single strongest discriminant available (\S\ref{sec:predecessor-compare}).

Each band keeps its native catalogue calibration. Optical magnitudes use their
survey conventions; AllWISE and VHS retain Vega zero points; Legacy fluxes,
including $W1,W2$, are AB nanomaggies. A native magnitude of $22.5$ corresponds
to flux one. AllWISE profile-fit DN fluxes use the documented zero points
$20.5$, $19.5$, $18.0$ and $13.0$ for $W1$--$W4$. Magnitude-only tables supply
measured magnitudes and errors; an absent magnitude is never converted into an
invented flux or an upper limit. Photometry is \emph{observed}, not dereddened:
the training samples and the field sample are at $|b|\ge25^\circ$, where the
correction is small, and a candidate must be supplied the same way.

A band is usable only when its survey says so: finite flux and positive
variance, a positive exposure count where the survey reports one, and the
survey's cleanliness flag (Legacy \texttt{maskbits}$=0$, SDSS \texttt{clean},
NSC \texttt{flags}$=0$, SkyMapper per-band \texttt{ngood} and \texttt{flags},
VHS per-band error bits, AllWISE \texttt{cc\_flags}). An unusable band enters
the observed-dimension mask and is marginalised exactly.

\subsection{Luptitudes and their covariance}
\label{sec:luptitudes}

For native calibrated flux $f_b$ we use the asinh magnitude
%
\begin{equation}
u_b=22.5-\frac{2.5}{\ln 10}
 \left[\operatorname{asinh}\left(\frac{f_b}{2s_b}\right)+\ln s_b\right],
\label{eq:luptitude}
\end{equation}
%
with a softening $s_b$ per band equal to the median flux error of the band in
the training field sample, saved with the model. Luptitudes approach ordinary
magnitudes at high signal-to-noise and stay finite for zero and negative flux.
That matters: a faint source can have a negative measured flux, and that is a
real measurement --- faintness in WISE is among the strongest signals that
something is \emph{not} a quasar. Dropping such measurements, or replacing them
with upper limits, would remove the most informative part of the competing
population.

The covariance follows from the Jacobian
$\partial u_b/\partial f_b = -(2.5/\ln10)/\sqrt{f_b^2+4s_b^2}$ applied to the full
flux covariance, including any off-diagonal measurement correlations the caller
supplies. This is first order; it is accurate while the flux error is small
compared with $\sqrt{f_b^2+4s_b^2}$, which the softening guarantees at low flux.

\subsection{Conditioning on a reference band}
\label{sec:reference}

A density over all bands, brightness included, has the brightness distribution
of the training sample inside it --- a property of the target selection, not of
quasar colour. Conditioning on one measured band removes it. Let $O$ be the
observed bands and $a\in O$ the first of them in a saved priority order
(SDSS $r$, Legacy south $r$, Legacy north $r$, PS1 $r$, NSC $r$, SkyMapper
$r$, VHS $J$, AllWISE $W1$, then the rest). For a quasar slice,
%
\begin{equation}
p(\uvec_{O\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z)
=\frac{p(\uvec_{O}\mid Q,z)}{p(u_a\mid Q,z)},
\label{eq:conditional}
\end{equation}
%
both obtained from the joint mixture after convolving the candidate's
measurement covariance, so the reference band's own error is included and its
correlation with the others is respected. Conditioning the whole mixture also
re-weights its components; conditioning only the component means would be a
different, incorrect density. At fixed $u_a$, replacing the other luptitudes by
the colours $u_b-u_a$ has unit Jacobian, so \eqref{eq:conditional} is a colour
density. Neighbouring redshift slices are interpolated in conditional density,
which preserves normalisation. The field densities are conditioned on the same
band.

The brightness itself enters only through the surface densities
$\Sq(z,u_a)$ and $\Sb(u_a)$, which is where it belongs: how many quasars and
field sources exist at this brightness is a population statement, and the
Bayes factor \eqref{eq:bf} stays free of it.

\subsection{Missing bands}

A Gaussian mixture marginalises exactly over unobserved dimensions: delete the
corresponding rows and columns of every component covariance and of the
observation's covariance. No imputation is involved; the result is the
likelihood of what was measured. Two measured bands supply one colour and can
be scored when the caller asks for a minimum of two; a richer input is evaluated
jointly, so separate survey Bayes factors are never multiplied under an
independence assumption.

\section{The quasar model}
\label{sec:qso}

\subsection{Training sample}
\label{sec:qso-sample}

The parent is DESI DR1 (\texttt{spectype='QSO'}, \texttt{zwarn=0}) and SDSS
DR16Q \citep{lyke2020dr16q}, de-duplicated by position at $1''$ --- by
position, not by identifier, because DESI DR1 alone repeats $9{,}577$ quasars
under two target identifiers --- and restricted to $|b|\ge25^\circ$:
$1{,}856{,}961$ quasars. From it we draw up to $2{,}000$ per redshift interval of
width $0.1$ over $0.1<z<4.4$ (fewer in the four sparsest intervals), $82{,}489$
in all, and match each to the seven catalogues within $1''$ ($2''$ for AllWISE).
The draw is flat in redshift so that every slice is equally well constrained;
within an interval it is uniform, so the brightness and colour distributions
there are those of the parent. The sixteen nside-$4$ sky blocks reserved by the
predecessor model are reserved again before anything is fitted, so the two can
be compared on identical held-out objects; fifteen of them contain drawn quasars,
$14{,}814$ in all ($14{,}766$ with at least two usable bands). Component counts
are chosen on sixteen further blocks inside the training sample, and $67{,}574$
quasars are fitted.

DESI targets quasars with a classifier built on these very colours, so the
training set describes $p(\uvec\mid Q,z,\text{selected})$. SDSS, whose targeting
channels differ, supplies $18\%$ of the drawn sample.

Figure~\ref{fig:locus} shows the problem the quasar model has to solve, in
Legacy luptitude colours. Quasars trace a track through
colour space that moves with redshift, doubles back on itself, and crosses the
locus of ordinary catalogue sources more than once; the motion is driven by
strong emission lines and the Lyman break entering and leaving the passbands.

\subsection{Extreme deconvolution}
\label{sec:xd}

Each object is measured with its own heteroscedastic, correlated error. Fitting
a mixture to the observed points would recover the locus \emph{convolved} with
those errors, which is systematically too wide, and the width is what
discriminates. We fit the \emph{intrinsic} distribution by extreme
deconvolution \citep{bovy2011xd}: a Gaussian mixture
%
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{k} \alpha_k\,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{v}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\mathbf{V}_k)
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
%
whose components are convolved with each observation's covariance
$\mathbf{S}_i$ during fitting, so that the observed value is evaluated with
$\mathbf{V}_k+\mathbf{S}_i$. The update is given in Appendix~\ref{app:xd}.
Figure~\ref{fig:deconv} tests the principle on real quasar colours by injecting
noise of known size: the deconvolved width stays within $8\%$ of the truth up to
an added noise of $1.5$ times the intrinsic width, and $14\%$ low at twice it,
while an ordinary mixture tracks the broadened distribution.

\paragraph{Whose implementation.} The algorithm is Bovy, Hogg \& Roweis's; the
code is not. \texttt{qso\_pcolor} carries its own numpy implementation, because
we wanted marginalisation over an absent band to be exact and per object: a
boolean mask selecting each object's observed sub-block, where the reference
implementation uses per-object projection matrices. With no missing dimensions
the two agree by construction. Two defects were found and fixed during
development --- a covariance M step that cancelled catastrophically at flux
scales of $10^8$, and a transposed cross term that corrupted off-diagonal
covariances only when bands were missing --- and both are pinned by regression
tests against independent routes (quadrature, Monte Carlo, closed forms).

\subsection{Redshift slices rather than one joint mixture}
\label{sec:slices}

One could fit a single mixture to $[\uvec,z]$ and condition on $z$, as XDQSOz
does \citep{bovy2012xdqsoz}. Within a component the colour--redshift relation
is then linear, so the strongly curved quasar track --- emission lines and the
Lyman break entering and leaving the passbands --- needs components narrow in
$z$, and conditioning on $z$ re-weights components by their $z$-marginal, the
redshift distribution of the training sample. We instead fit an independent
deconvolved mixture in each of $43$ overlapping slices, centred from
$z=0.15$ to $4.35$ in steps of $0.1$, each using quasars within half a slice
width beyond its edges, and interpolate linearly in density:
%
\begin{equation}
p(\uvec\mid Q,z) = (1-w)\,p_{j}(\uvec) + w\,p_{j+1}(\uvec),
\qquad
w = \frac{z-z_j}{z_{j+1}-z_j}.
\label{eq:slices}
\end{equation}
%
Each $p_j$ is normalised, so \eqref{eq:slices} is a normalised density for any
$z$, and the training sample's redshift distribution \emph{between} slices
cannot leak into it; the redshift prior is supplied separately and visibly, as
$\Sq$. Within a slice the fit is still an average over the training redshifts
there. Component counts are chosen per slice by held-out density on the
selection blocks from $K\in\{1,2,4\}$: $K=4$ in $33$ slices and $K=2$ in $10$.
Outside $0.15\le z\le4.35$ the model has no slice; the scorer integrates only
inside that support and reports, as \texttt{frac\_norm\_outside\_support}, how
much of the redshift normalisation a wider grid would have taken from outside
it.

The fits stop at a $150$-iteration cap; one of $43$ reaches the $10^{-5}$
tolerance. The validation below evaluates the saved fits at that stopping point.

Figure~\ref{fig:slices} shows the fitted density as probability contours in
the Legacy $g-r$, $r-z$ plane at three redshifts, conditioned on a reference
luptitude; Figure~\ref{fig:modelz} shows it as a function of redshift in four
Legacy colours against the data it was fitted to.

\section{The field model}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Everything a chance neighbour can be}

The competing density is built from the surveys themselves. We take $24$ cones
of radius $0.15^\circ$ at $|b|\ge25^\circ$, spread in declination and over
distinct sky blocks, query all seven catalogues in each, and form the positional
union: sources are associated across catalogues within $1''$ ($2''$ for
AllWISE), one-to-one and nearest first, and an unmatched source is kept as a
source of its own. No morphological or star/galaxy selection is applied, because
the candidates have none: any selection applied to the field sample that the
candidates do not share is a bias in the Bayes factor. The predecessor model
measured the cost of getting this wrong: a background built from point sources
alone made galaxies look like a serious contaminant (quasar/galaxy AUC $0.80$),
while an all-source background gave $0.96$ (Appendix~\ref{app:predecessor}).

\paragraph{Known quasars are removed, and it matters more than it looks.}
$\Hfield$ and $\Hbkg$ must be mutually exclusive. Quasars are a tiny fraction of
all sources, but what matters is their fraction \emph{in quasar-like colour
space}, where the same-redshift hypothesis is tested. Measured on a one-degree
Legacy cone with the predecessor model:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
$\ln \mathrm{BF}$ (quasar at $z_0$ / background) & sources &
known quasars \\
\midrule
$-40$ to $-10$ & $17{,}995$ & $0.1\%$ \\
$-5$ to $-2$   & $6{,}026$  & $0.5\%$ \\
$0$ to $+2$    & $978$      & $9.5\%$ \\
$+2$ to $+5$   & $725$      & $31.2\%$ \\
$+5$ to $+40$  & $272$      & $\mathbf{72.4\%}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Left in, the field model learns the quasar locus and competes against the
quasar hypothesis using quasars, costing about $1.3$ in $\ln\mathrm{BF}$.
DESI DR1 and SDSS DR16Q quasars within $1''$ are therefore removed from every
field cone. Unobserved quasars remain, a residual of the same bias in the same
direction; they are meant to be carried by $\Sq$ through a completeness
(\S\ref{sec:priors}).

After quality cuts and quasar removal the fields hold $174{,}567$ sources with at
least two usable bands; six whole fields ($42{,}493$ sources) are reserved for
validation, four more choose the number of components, and a further reserved
overlap field ($6{,}825$ sources) supplies survey combinations absent from the
six.

Figure~\ref{fig:densities} shows the two class-conditional densities in the
same colour plane and the resulting colour Bayes factor. The populations
overlap; the method does not pretend otherwise, and the Bayes factor varies
smoothly rather than drawing a decision boundary.

\subsection{Rare measurement patterns}

Which bands a field source has depends on where it is and how bright it is,
and some combinations are rare. The fit uses $20{,}000$ training sources drawn
so that every observed-band pattern is represented by at least $20$ sources
where it has them, with inverse sampling weights restoring each pattern's
abundance in the full field sample. This keeps rare combinations in the fit
without promoting them in the field distribution. Component selection over
$K\in\{4,8,12\}$ chooses $K=8$; the fit stops at the $150$-iteration cap.

\subsection{Dedicated fits for Legacy-only input}
\label{sec:marginals}

A single $41$-band mixture of eight components is a coarse description of a
field population that varies a great deal between surveys' depths, and it
under-represents the stellar locus when only Legacy bands are supplied. The
model therefore also stores separately fitted joint densities over two band
sets: Legacy south $g,r,z$ and Legacy south $g,r,z,W1,W2$, each fitted to the
same weighted training rows ($13{,}239$ sources with all of its bands, $2{,}597$
of them in the selection fields), with its component count chosen on those
fields from $K\in\{8,16,32\}$: both choose $K=32$, by $0.001$ and $0.016$ nats
per object over $K=16$, and both converge (in $60$ and $84$ iterations). A
dedicated fit is used only when
\emph{every} observed band lies in its band set, the smallest covering set
first; otherwise the $41$-band background is used. Routing depends on which
bands were measured, never on their values or on class. Each dedicated fit is a
normalised density conditioned on the reference band exactly as
\eqref{eq:conditional}, and is an alternative fitted approximation, not the
projection of the joint one. The quasar density always uses the joint quasar
mixtures.

The effect is measured on the labelled pairs of \S\ref{sec:validation}. With
$g,r,z$ alone, the dedicated fit raises the held-out quasar/star AUC from $0.795$
under the $41$-band background to $0.874$. With $g,r,z,W1,W2$ it moves
quasar/star from $0.981$ to $0.982$ and quasar/galaxy from $0.956$ to $0.961$,
and lowers the same-/wrong-redshift AUC by $\ln R$ from $0.833$ to $0.828$:
a better field density enters the denominator of $R$ for quasars too.

\section{Surface densities, per reference band}
\label{sec:priors}

$\Sq(z,u_a)$ and $\Sb(u_a)$ must be densities per unit native luptitude of the
reference band, for objects \emph{with that band measured}, because that is the
condition under which the conditional densities of \S\ref{sec:reference} are
evaluated. They are built separately for each of the $41$ bands
(\path{scripts/build_multisurvey_priors.py}); $37$ have enough data, and a
candidate whose reference is one of the other four (NSC $u$, SkyMapper $u,v$,
VHS $Y$) receives the no-prior status, with evidence but no posterior.

\paragraph{Footprints.} A band can be missing because the object is outside the
survey or because it is too faint. Only the second is information about the
population, so both densities are counted inside the footprint of the survey
that supplies the reference band. For the field cones the footprint is
unambiguous: a survey's detected fraction in a cone is either zero or not
(measured). For quasars, a cell of HEALPix nside $32$ ($3.4\,{\rm deg}^2$) is in
the footprint if the survey detected at least two quasars in it; shallow
surveys such as SkyMapper and VHS miss many faint quasars even inside their
footprint, which is why presence rather than a detected fraction defines it.

\paragraph{Quasars.} Each quasar of the flat draw is weighted by
$N_{\rm parent}(\text{bin})/n_{\rm drawn}(\text{bin})$, which restores the
parent's redshift distribution exactly (the weights sum to the $1{,}856{,}961$
parent quasars); within a redshift bin the draw is uniform, so the brightness
distribution is the parent's. The weighted counts in (redshift $0.1$,
luptitude $0.5$) cells, inside the footprint, are divided by the footprint's
share of the parent coverage ($13{,}825\,{\rm deg}^2$ in all; $10{,}048$ for
Legacy south):
%
\begin{equation}
\Sq(z,u_a) = C\,\frac{\sum_i w_i\,[\,z_i,u_{a,i}\in\text{cell},\ a\text{ measured},\
             i\in\text{footprint}_a]}{A_a\,\Delta z\,\Delta u}.
\label{eq:sigma_q}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{One completeness constant.} The parent is spectroscopically
incomplete, unevenly over the sky. We apply one constant $C$ to every band,
fixed so that the Legacy-south $r$ density integrates over $17\le u_r<22.5$ to
the predecessor's validated prior, which was normalised to DESI's best-covered
sky: $C=2.30$. That assumes the completeness does not depend on which band is the
reference.

\paragraph{Field.} $\Sb(u_a)$ is the number of clean field sources with band $a$
measured, per unit $u_a$, divided by the area of the cones that survey covers.

\paragraph{Checks.} The Legacy-south values reproduce the predecessor's on
different data. Its independently counted field density over $17\le r<22.5$ is
$18{,}945\,{\rm deg}^{-2}$, against $20{,}661$ from eight $0.5^\circ$ Legacy cones
with dereddened photometry ($-8\%$, of the expected sign: dereddening brightens
sources past the limit). Quasar densities seen through different surveys' $r$
bands agree without being forced to:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
reference band & $\Sq$, $17\le u_a<22.5$ (deg$^{-2}$) & $\Sb$, same range (deg$^{-2}$) \\
\midrule
Legacy south $r$ & $254.9$ & $18{,}945$ \\
Legacy north $r$ & $212.1$ & $13{,}415$ \\
SDSS $r$         & $245.1$ & $12{,}312$ \\
PS1 $r$          & $263.0$ & $15{,}021$ \\
NSC $r$          & $216.1$ & $12{,}577$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The field densities differ, as they should: $\Sb$ counts sources \emph{measured}
in that band, and the shallower surveys measure fewer. A unit test requires the
quasar densities over $17\le u_a<21$ to agree within $20\%$.

\paragraph{What a completeness does and does not change.} Multiplying $\Sq$ by a
constant leaves $p(z\in W\mid\uvec,Q)$ unchanged, because both quasar terms scale
together, but not the competition with the field: with total quasar intensity
$Q$ and field intensity $B$, $p_{\rm same}$ and $R$ change by $a(Q+B)/(aQ+B)$,
which depends on the candidate. A redshift-\emph{dependent} completeness changes
the balance between $\lambda_{\rm same}$ and $\lambda_{\rm field}$ as well:
intensities $(1,1,1)$ give $p_{\rm same}=1/3$, while a correction multiplying
only the field term by $100$ gives $1/102$. $C$ is a declared, reproducible
choice, not a conservative one. Where no prior pair exists for the reference
band, the scorer returns the evidence and leaves the posterior fields null with
a status code, rather than substituting a class fraction.

\section{The redshift window is a multiplicative constant}
\label{sec:window}

This section contains the main methodological point.

\subsection{The tension}

A physically motivated pair window is of order $\pm2000\kms$; at $z_0=1.4$
%
\begin{equation}
\Delta z = (1+z_0)\,\frac{\Delta v}{c}
         = 2.4\times\frac{2000}{299792.458} = 0.016 .
\end{equation}
%
Broadband photometry constrains a quasar redshift far more loosely, and often
to a multimodal distribution --- which is why XDQSOz produces a redshift
\emph{PDF} \citep{bovy2012xdqsoz}. With Legacy $g,r,z,W1,W2$, over $1500$
quasars from the reserved sky blocks at all redshifts, the quasar-only posterior
under a flat prior has median width $\sigma_z=0.42$ ($16$--$84$th percentiles
$0.23$--$0.72$); the median distance from the posterior mean to the
spectroscopic redshift is $0.17$. At $z_0=1.8$ the window spans $0.037$ in
redshift; for a unimodal posterior of width $0.42$ it captures of order three
per cent of the probability (Figure~\ref{fig:window}). So $\lambda_{\rm field}\gg\lambda_{\rm
same}$ and $p_{\rm same}$ is small even for an object exactly on the locus at
exactly the right redshift. That is the correct answer to the question as posed,
and it means the question as posed is not the one colours can answer.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{../../plots/method/model_window.png}
\caption{\emph{Top:} quasar-only redshift posteriors $p(z\mid\uvec,Q)$ under a
flat prior for three quasars from the reserved sky blocks with
$z_{\rm spec}\approx1.8$, from their Legacy $g,r,z,W1,W2$: the width, and a
secondary solution the colours cannot rule out --- one of the three peaks at
$z\approx2.2$. \emph{Bottom:} that posterior with the $\pm2000\kms$ window drawn
to scale (orange band). The kinks at intervals of $0.1$ are real: the model
interpolates linearly in density between independently fitted slices of two or
four components, so the likelihood is piecewise linear in $z$ with changes of
slope at the slice centres.}
\label{fig:window}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Which uncertainty is the problem}
\label{sec:whichsigma}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & $\Delta z$ at $z=1.8$ & relative \\
\midrule
primary's spectroscopic error        & $0.0004$ & $1\times$ \\
$\pm2000\kms$ window (half-width)    & $0.019$  & $49\times$ \\
companion's photometric width        & $0.42$   & $1050\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The median DESI DR1 quasar redshift error is $0.0004$ ($43\kms$). The primary is
known some $1000$ times better than the companion; the difficulty sits entirely
on the companion. What the primary's precision buys is that
$p(\uvec\mid Q,z_0)$ is evaluated at a sharp point. Its error enters by
\emph{marginalising} the window over it, which smooths the window's edges while
conserving its area; an earlier implementation replaced the half-width by
$\sqrt{s^2+\sigma_{\rm primary}^2}$, which increased the area, so a less certain
primary produced more same-redshift companions. The $43\kms$ is a pipeline
statistical error; quasar systemic redshifts from broad lines, C\,\textsc{iv} in
particular, carry systematic offsets that are a non-trivial fraction of a pair
window, and \texttt{z\_primary\_err} should be set to a systemic uncertainty
measured for the estimator actually used.

\subsection{What sets the companion's width}
\label{sec:whatsets}

To first order
%
\begin{equation}
\sigma_z \;\approx\;
\frac{\text{scatter of colour at fixed } z}
     {\left|\mathrm{d}(\text{colour})/\mathrm{d}z\right|} .
\label{eq:sigmaz}
\end{equation}
%
In the model near $z=1.8$, Legacy $g-r$ moves at $0.29$ per unit redshift with
intrinsic scatter $0.23$, and $r-z$ at $0.31$ with $0.22$: implied widths $0.8$
and $0.7$ from each colour alone; combined with $z-W1$ and $W1-W2$ the posterior
width is $0.42$ (\S\ref{sec:window}).
At bright magnitudes the scatter is intrinsic quasar-to-quasar diversity, not
measurement error, so deeper imaging in the same bands buys little. Fifteen
times more training data left $\sigma_z$ unchanged to $0.001$
(Appendix~\ref{app:predecessor}). Improving the redshift constraint needs bands
in which quasar colour changes \emph{faster} with redshift: a $u$ band spanning
the Lyman break, or a narrow band on a strong line. This is part of the case for
combining surveys, and Table~\ref{tab:subsets} shows that combinations
including SDSS $u$ discriminate redshift better.

\subsection{Factorising the window out}

Because the window is much narrower than the scale on which
$\Sq\,p(\uvec\mid Q,z)$ varies, \eqref{eq:lam_same} becomes
%
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\rm same} \simeq
\Delta Z_{\rm eff}\;\Sq(z_0,u_a)\;p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z_0),
\qquad
\Delta Z_{\rm eff} \equiv \int W(z\mid z_0)\,\dd z .
\label{eq:narrow}
\end{equation}
%
The step that makes this clean is the denominator. $\Hsame$ and $\Hfield$
\emph{partition} the quasar intensity, so
%
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\rm same}+\lambda_{\rm field}+\lambda_{\rm bkg}+\lambda_{\rm out}
= \Lambda_{Q,{\rm tot}} + \lambda_{\rm bkg}+\lambda_{\rm out}
\qquad\text{for \emph{any} window,}
\label{eq:partition}
\end{equation}
%
with $\Lambda_{Q,{\rm tot}}=\int\Sq\,p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z)\,\dd z$.
Defining
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
R \;\equiv\;
\frac{\Sq(z_0,u_a)\;p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z_0)}
     {\Lambda_{Q,{\rm tot}} + \lambda_{\rm bkg}+\lambda_{\rm out}}
\;}
\qquad [\text{redshift}^{-1}]
\label{eq:R}
\end{equation}
%
gives
%
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm same} = R\;\Delta Z_{\rm eff},
\label{eq:linear}
\end{equation}
%
a linear relation, not an odds transformation.

Two cautions. First, \eqref{eq:partition} is exact but \eqref{eq:narrow} is an
approximation: writing $f(z)=\Sq(z,u_a)\,p(\uvec_{\setminus a}\mid u_a,Q,z)$,
%
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm same}
= R(z_0)\,\Delta Z_{\rm eff}
+ \frac{\int W(z)\,[f(z)-f(z_0)]\,\dd z}{D},
\qquad D = \int f\,\dd z + \lambda_{\rm bkg}+\lambda_{\rm out},
\end{equation}
%
so the linear relation holds to the extent that $f$ is flat across the window.
The leading relative correction is $f''(z_0)s^2/[6f(z_0)]$ for a top hat of
half-width $s$; at a cusp of the piecewise-linear interpolation it is $O(s)$.
Second, the implementation stores the \emph{window-averaged} $R =
p_{\rm same}/\Delta Z_{\rm eff}$, so \eqref{eq:linear} holds for the reported
numbers by construction. The substantive claim is that $R$ does not depend on
the window, which holds in the narrow limit: over a factor of ten in window
width $R$ moves by $2\times10^{-5}$ of its value (Figure~\ref{fig:factorisation}).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../plots/method/model_factorisation.png}
\caption{The factorisation on a reserved quasar at $z\approx1.8$ scored from its
Legacy $g,r,z,W1,W2$ with the priors and the unmodelled term, for windows from
$\pm200$ to $\pm20{,}000\kms$. \emph{Left:} $p_{\rm same}$ is proportional to the
window area, as \eqref{eq:linear} requires; the dotted line is $R\,\Delta Z$ with

$0.004\le\Delta Z_{\rm eff}\le0.04$ --- velocity windows up to $\pm2000\kms$
and beyond --- it changes by $2\times10^{-5}$ of its value, and departs only when
the window stops being narrow compared with $\sigma_z$.}
\label{fig:factorisation}
\end{figure}

\subsection{How much discrimination is actually available}
\label{sec:howmuch}

Figure~\ref{fig:separation} evaluates $R$ against a primary at $z_0=1.8$ for
three populations from the reserved sky blocks and cones, scored from Legacy
$g,r,z,W1,W2$ with the priors and the unmodelled term:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
population & $n$ & median $\ln R$ \\
\midrule
quasars with $|z-z_0|<0.05$   & $282$ & $-0.2$ \\
quasars with $|z-z_0|>0.6$    & $397$ & $-4.2$ \\
field sources, $19.5<u_r<21.5$ & $400$ & $-14.3$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two very different gaps. Against the field population the statistic is
decisive: $e^{14.1}\approx10^{6}$. Against \emph{quasars at the wrong redshift}
it is worth a factor of about $55$ ($e^{4.0}$). That asymmetry is a statement
about the information in $g,r,z,W1,W2$: these bands identify quasars very well
and place them in redshift only weakly, as the overlapping loci of
Figure~\ref{fig:slices} and the posterior widths of Figure~\ref{fig:window} show.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{What this method delivers reliably is a quasar classification},
      not a redshift match. A companion with high $R$ is very likely a quasar;
      whether it is at $z_0$ is a much weaker inference.
\item \textbf{The field-quasar hypothesis is therefore the dominant competing
      explanation}, which is why a quasar-versus-star calculation would be
      misleading rather than merely incomplete.
\item \textbf{Improving redshift discrimination requires more information, not
      more training data} (Appendix~\ref{app:predecessor}: fifteen times more
      quasars left $\sigma_z$ unchanged). What helps is a band in which quasar
      colour changes faster with redshift --- SDSS $u$ across the Lyman break
      (Table~\ref{tab:subsets}) --- or variability, or spectroscopy.
\end{itemize}

These are medians of a controlled comparison with the hardest intermediate
redshifts absent; purity and completeness are measured on the labelled pairs of
\S\ref{sec:validation}.

\subsection{Consequences}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Rank on $R$.} It is the window-free evidence, so two people can
      compare candidates without agreeing on a window, and it is not compressed
      towards zero the way $p_{\rm same}$ is.
\item \textbf{Never quote $p_{\rm same}$ without its window.} Every output row
      carries both, as \texttt{p\_sameq} and \texttt{dz\_match\_eff}.
\item \textbf{Never integrate a velocity window on the main redshift grid.}
      $\Delta Z_{\rm eff}=0.03$ on a grid of step $0.01$ is two or three points.
      The scorer inserts dense window nodes, exact support boundaries and the
      model and prior interpolation knots into one quadrature grid shared by the
      window and the total quasar intensity, so a prior peak resolved by one is
      resolved by both, and nothing is integrated outside the common support.
\item \textbf{Widening the window does not improve the answer.} It changes the
      question.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Colour outliers: the unmodelled hypothesis}
\label{sec:outliers}

\subsection{The defect}

Both colour densities are Gaussian mixtures convolved with the object's
covariance $\mathbf S$. Far from every component of both, each is dominated by
its widest component in the direction of the displacement $\Delta\uvec$, and
their log ratio becomes a difference of quadratic forms,
%
\begin{equation}
\ln\frac{p(\uvec\mid Q,z)}{p(\uvec\mid B)} \;\approx\;
-\tfrac12\,\Delta\uvec^{T}\Bigl[(\mathbf V_Q+\mathbf S)^{-1}
-(\mathbf V_B+\mathbf S)^{-1}\Bigr]\Delta\uvec \;+\;\text{const},
\label{eq:tailratio}
\end{equation}
%
which grows without bound in any direction where the widest quasar component is
wider than the widest field component. Neither model was fitted to objects
there, so \eqref{eq:tailratio} reflects how two fits' tails fell, not evidence.
With three hypotheses an artefact, a moving object or a population absent from
both training sets is called a quasar with probability one.

Measured on the model with three hypotheses: the README candidate
($z_0=1.8$, $u_r\approx21$, Legacy $g,r,z,W1,W2$) with its $g$ flux multiplied
by $8$ at fixed error lies $4.8\sigma$ from every quasar component and
$7.3\sigma$ from every field component, and is a quasar with probability
$1.000$, $\ln\mathrm{BF}=+14.4$; multiplied by $32$, $7.5\sigma$ and $11.2\sigma$
away, likewise. Among the $21{,}284$ spectroscopic non-quasars of the pair
validation, $95$ lie more than $3\sigma$ from both models, and $17$ of those are
called quasars, against $0.8\%$ within $2\sigma$ (Table~\ref{tab:sweep},
Figure~\ref{fig:outliers}).


Two distances identify the regime and are reported for every object: the
Mahalanobis distance to the nearest component,
%
\begin{equation}
d = \min_k \sqrt{(\uvec-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^T(\mathbf V_k+\mathbf S)^{-1}
    (\uvec-\boldsymbol\mu_k)},
\label{eq:ood}
\end{equation}
%
conditioned on the reference band, over every component of every quasar slice
($d_Q$, \texttt{qso\_ood\_sigma\_any\_z}) and over the field density actually
used ($d_B$, \texttt{bkg\_ood\_sigma}). The distance to the $z_0$ slice alone,
\texttt{qso\_ood\_sigma}, is large for every wrong-redshift quasar and cannot
identify this regime. $\min(d_Q,d_B)$ large means every density in the
calculation is an extrapolation; the flag \texttt{outside\_both\_models} is
raised above a threshold the caller must state.

\subsection{A fourth hypothesis}

The field is written as a modelled part and a broad unmodelled part $U$ --- the
standard device for data containing points no component describes
\citep[\S3]{hogg2010}:
%
\begin{equation}
p(\uvec\mid\text{field}) = (1-\eta)\,p(\uvec\mid B) + \eta\,p(\uvec\mid U),
\qquad
p(\uvec\mid U)=t_\nu\bigl(\uvec\mid\bar\uvec,\;c^2\boldsymbol\Sigma_B+\mathbf S\bigr),
\label{eq:field_u}
\end{equation}
%
a multivariate Student-t over all $41$ bands with $\nu$ degrees of freedom,
centred on the field and with scale $c^2$ times the field's own covariance
$\boldsymbol\Sigma_B$, conditioned on the reference band like everything else.
Marginals of a $t$ are $t$ with the same $\nu$ and the sub-block of the scale,
so missing bands are marginalised exactly and the conditional is the joint over
the reference marginal, in closed form. The object's covariance $\mathbf S$ is
added to the scale, which is exact only in the Gaussian limit; the tail, which
is the point of $U$, is unaffected. $U$ gives $\lambda_{\rm out}$ of
\eqref{eq:lam_out}, which joins every denominator, and the Bayes factor
\eqref{eq:bf} compares against the whole field. With $\eta=0$ the scorer reduces
exactly to three hypotheses (a unit test).

\paragraph{Why a heavy tail, and not a wide Gaussian.} $U$ must eventually
exceed every Gaussian component of both models, in every direction. A $t$ does
so whatever its scale, because its density falls as a power of the distance and
every Gaussian's falls exponentially. A Gaussian $U$ can do it only by being
wider than every component in every direction, and a first version was built
that way: an anisotropic envelope $\mathbf E\succeq\mathbf V_k$ for all $224$
components, obtained by bounding each whitened component by its absolute row
sums in a common eigenbasis. It worked where both models are hopeless --- no
non-quasar more than $3\sigma$ from both was then called a quasar --- but not in
between. A few components are very wide in some band, so the envelope, projected
onto Legacy $g,r,z$ at $u_r=20.8$, had standard deviations of $3.8$ and $5.0$
luptitudes against $\sim0.5$ for the field; its fitted share, spread over that
volume, contributed a density of $e^{-10.5}$ at its peak. Where the quasar
density was a per cent of its peak and the field model's far below, the ratio of
two Gaussian tails still decided: $28\%$ of the plane with both densities below
$1\%$ of their peaks had $\ln\mathrm{BF}>0$, and $6\%$ had $\ln\mathrm{BF}>5$
(Figure~\ref{fig:densities}). Building the envelope from the observed bands'
blocks alone narrowed it only to $2.6\times2.9$ luptitudes and left the fraction
at $26\%$.

\paragraph{The field model's own tails are measurably wrong.} Bright field
sources ($\sigma<0.03$ in $g$ and $z$) within $0.25$ of a reference $u_r$,
counted where the field model's density is a given fraction of its peak, against

(\path{scripts/fit_multisurvey_outlier.py}). The fitting set (A) is the

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Nothing above is evidence that the method works. That requires labelled
objects, and two kinds are used: pairs in which both members have spectra, and
reserved quasars and field sources for every survey combination.

\subsection{Spectroscopic pairs: design}

Around each DESI DR1 quasar we take every companion at $3$--$30''$ that has a
DR1 spectrum of its own and label it by what that spectrum says: a quasar within
$\pm3000\kms$ of the primary (\textsf{same\_z}), a quasar outside
(\textsf{field\_q}), or anything else (\textsf{non\_qso}). The labels are
spectroscopic and so independent of the photometry being scored. The
\emph{inclusion} of an object is not: DESI targets quasars with a classifier
built on these colours, so the labelled set is a colour-selected subset of the
sky, and its calibration transfers only to candidates drawn the same way. The
non-quasars in particular are DESI targets, not a random draw of the imaging.

From $1{,}645{,}842$ DESI DR1 quasar primaries, $579{,}572$ companions have a
usable spectrum. We require Legacy south photometry, a primary inside the
trained redshift support, \texttt{maskbits}$=0$, reference-band
\texttt{fracflux\_r}$\le0.2$, $17\le r<22.5$ and usable $g,r,z$; the non-quasars
are subsampled to $30{,}000$ with seed zero. That leaves $50{,}746$ companions:
$3{,}308$ \textsf{same\_z}, $26{,}154$ \textsf{field\_q}, $21{,}284$
\textsf{non\_qso}. The $14{,}221$ in the sixteen reserved sky blocks --- $880$,
$7{,}293$ and $6{,}048$ --- are held out from the quasar fit; ranking AUCs use
them. Every companion is scored from its Legacy $g,r,z,W1,W2$ fluxes as observed
(\path{scripts/validate_pairs_multisurvey.py}).

\paragraph{Redshift discrimination.} Ranking the held-out quasar companions by
$\ln R$, the AUC for \textsf{same\_z} against \textsf{field\_q} is
$\mathbf{0.828}$; by $p(z\in W\mid\uvec,Q)$ alone --- the quasar-only ranking,
which no field density enters --- it is $0.865$; by the Bayes factor, which has
no field-quasar term, $0.720$ (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}). This is
discrimination against the whole wrong-redshift population, not velocity
resolution.

\paragraph{Quasars just outside the window.} Against the $151$ held-out quasars
with $3000\le|\Delta v|<6000\kms$, the $880$ same-redshift quasars give AUC
$0.472$ by $\ln R$ --- chance. The photometry does not resolve the match boundary.
This is a limit of the information, not something refitting would change.

\paragraph{The quasar finder.} Against spectroscopic stars the Bayes factor has
AUC $\mathbf{0.982}$, and $0.04\%$ of stars exceed the median same-redshift
quasar; against spectroscopic galaxies, $\mathbf{0.961}$ and $0.18\%$. Against
all non-quasars, $0.966$; ranking by $\ln R$ instead, same-redshift quasars
against non-quasars, $0.995$.

\paragraph{Calibration, and what the miscalibration measures.} The predicted
$p(z\in W\mid\uvec,Q)$ is low by a factor that depends on separation:

\begin{tabular}{crccc}
\toprule
separation & $n$ & mean predicted & observed & ratio \\
\midrule
$3$--$5''$   & $674$      & $0.038$ & $0.368$ & $9.8$ \\
$5$--$10''$  & $2{,}754$  & $0.032$ & $0.235$ & $7.4$ \\
$10$--$20''$ & $9{,}912$  & $0.025$ & $0.123$ & $4.9$ \\
$20$--$30''$ & $16{,}122$ & $0.022$ & $0.074$ & $3.3$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The scorer draws the companion's redshift from the field distribution $\Sq$, but
quasars cluster, so a companion at $3''$ (about $25$~kpc at $z\sim1.5$) has an
excess probability of sharing the primary's redshift that the field prior does
not contain. The shape of the prediction is right --- the ranking says so --- and
its normalisation is short by the projected quasar--quasar correlation
integrated over the window. The excess is left out of the scorer on purpose, so
that the ranking carries no cosmological assumption; a user who wants a
probability multiplies the odds by the factor for their separation. The
predecessor measured the same factors to two significant figures ($10.0$, $7.4$,
$4.9$, $3.3$), as it should: the ratio measures the sky, not the model. Whether
it reaches unity beyond $30''$ is not yet measured.

\section{Scope and limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Blending is the binding constraint.} Below a few arcseconds the survey
model fits divide flux between overlapping sources, so the two sets of
photometry are neither independent nor individually reliable, and a
probability computed from them describes the deblender rather than the sky.
Every candidate scored for science passes an explicit separation floor and
blending limit; a candidate lacking those measurements is treated as blended.
Survey quality flags alone do not establish that a low-resolution infrared
measurement --- AllWISE in particular, with its $\sim6''$ beam --- separates a
close pair; contaminated bands must be marked unusable. Closer pairs need
image-level forced photometry.

\paragraph{$p_{\rm same}$ is not the probability of a physical pair.} It is a
classification posterior under field priors. A physical-pair calculation
additionally conditions the quasar intensity on angular separation through
small-scale quasar clustering \citep{hennawi2006,eftekharzadeh2017}. That layer
is deliberately separate; its size is measured in \S\ref{sec:validation-results},
and a user who wants a probability multiplies the odds by that factor for their
separation. The ranking statistic is unaffected.

\paragraph{Colour cannot resolve the velocity window.} Quasars just outside
$\pm3000\kms$ are indistinguishable from those inside (\S\ref{sec:validation-results}).
The ranking separates same-redshift quasars from the wrong-redshift population
as a whole, and must not be read as velocity resolution.

\paragraph{The field model is coarse and global.} Eight components over $41$
bands, from $24$ small fields at $|b|\ge25^\circ$, with dedicated fits only for
Legacy-only input. The predecessor measured that a local field model changes
Legacy scores by at most $0.2$~nats above $|b|=20^\circ$
(Appendix~\ref{app:predecessor}); nothing here tests the Galactic plane, and the
surface densities are global.

\paragraph{Colours unlike anything either model has seen.} The unmodelled
hypothesis stops such objects being called certain quasars, at the cost of
weaker Bayes factors for real quasars far from the field; it does not make the
scorer informative there. An object flagged \texttt{outside\_both\_models} needs
a spectrum or another look at its photometry.

\paragraph{Unused evidence.} Gaia parallax and proper motion settle the question
outright for bright companions. Variability, where it exists, is a further
factor. The likelihood is modular so that such a term can be added as a factor.

\section{Gaussian identities used}
\label{app:gauss}

For a mixture \eqref{eq:mixture} over a partitioned vector
$\mathbf{v}=[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]$ and an observation $\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{y}$
with covariance $\mathbf{S}_y$, the conditional is again a mixture, with weights
%
\begin{equation}
w_k \propto \alpha_k\,
\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b,k},
\mathbf{V}_{bb,k}+\mathbf{S}_y\bigr),
\end{equation}
%
and components
%
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{a\mid y,k} &= \boldsymbol{\mu}_{a,k}
  + \mathbf{V}_{ab,k}\bigl(\mathbf{V}_{bb,k}+\mathbf{S}_y\bigr)^{-1}
    (\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{b,k}),\\
\mathbf{V}_{a\mid y,k} &= \mathbf{V}_{aa,k}
  - \mathbf{V}_{ab,k}\bigl(\mathbf{V}_{bb,k}+\mathbf{S}_y\bigr)^{-1}
    \mathbf{V}_{ba,k}.
\end{align}
%
The conditional covariance is a Schur complement. For a multivariate $t$ the
marginal over a subset of dimensions is a $t$ with the same $\nu$ and the
sub-block of the scale, so a conditional is the joint over the marginal of the
conditioning block; this is how the unmodelled density is conditioned on the
reference band (\S\ref{sec:outliers}). Marginalising
over a subset of dimensions deletes the corresponding rows and columns. No
inverse is ever formed: every quadratic form and log determinant comes from a
Cholesky factor, and all densities are computed in log space.

\section{The extreme-deconvolution update}
\label{app:xd}

For observation $i$ with observed sub-vector $\mathbf{w}_i$, noise covariance
$\mathbf{S}_i$, and component $j$, write $O$ for the observed dimensions and
%
\begin{align}
\mathbf{T}_{ij} &= \mathbf{V}_j^{OO} + \mathbf{S}_i, &
q_{ij} &\propto \alpha_j\,\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{w}_i\mid
        \boldsymbol{\mu}_j^{O},\mathbf{T}_{ij}\bigr), \\
\mathbf{b}_{ij} &= \boldsymbol{\mu}_j
  + \mathbf{V}_j^{\cdot O}\mathbf{T}_{ij}^{-1}
    \bigl(\mathbf{w}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_j^{O}\bigr), &
\mathbf{B}_{ij} &= \mathbf{V}_j
  - \mathbf{V}_j^{\cdot O}\mathbf{T}_{ij}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_j^{O\cdot},
\end{align}
%
with the M step
%
\begin{equation}
\alpha_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i q_{ij},
\quad
\boldsymbol{\mu}_j = \frac{\sum_i q_{ij}\mathbf{b}_{ij}}{\sum_i q_{ij}},
\quad
\mathbf{V}_j = \frac{\sum_i q_{ij}\bigl[\mathbf{B}_{ij}
   + (\mathbf{b}_{ij}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_j)
     (\mathbf{b}_{ij}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_j)^{\!\top}\bigr]}{\sum_i q_{ij}} .
\end{equation}
%
With $\mathbf{S}_i=0$ and no missing dimensions this reduces to ordinary
Gaussian-mixture EM. Restricting $O$ per object is how missing bands are
handled. Sample weights multiply every sum; that is where the field sample's
pattern weights enter. $\mathbf{T}_{ij}^{-1}$ appears only inside solves against
a Cholesky factor.

\section{The predecessor model}
\label{app:predecessor}

The model replaces a Legacy-only one, retired on 2026-09-26 and kept in
\path{models/archive/original_legacy_south/} because the model's own provenance
depends on it: its sixteen reserved sky blocks are reserved in the model too, and
its quasar prior fixes the model's completeness constant $C$
(\S\ref{sec:priors}).
\path{tests/test_archived_original.py} keeps its numbers reproducible.

\paragraph{What it was.} Dereddened Legacy DR9 south $g,r,z$ and forced $W1,W2$,
as flux ratios $f_b/f_r$ --- the XDQSO representation \citep{bovy2011xdqso},
which keeps negative fluxes without a transform --- with the Jacobian
covariance $[\mathbf C]_{jk}=\delta_{jk}v_j/f_r^2+r_jr_kv_r/f_r^2$; $43$ redshift
slices of up to $20$ components fitted to $1{,}106{,}986$ quasars ($917{,}489$
DESI, $189{,}497$ SDSS); a footprint-average field model, one mixture of eight
components per $r$ bin, fitted to all sources in eight $0.5^\circ$ cones at
$|b|>32^\circ$; surface densities $\Sq(z,r)$ from DESI quasars normalised to the
best-covered sky and $\Sb(r)$ from those cones; and, from 2026-09-26, the same
unmodelled hypothesis in its four flux-ratio coordinates.

\paragraph{What it measured, and still stands.} On the pairs of
\S\ref{sec:validation}, held-out AUC $0.815$ by $\ln R$, $0.855$ by
$p(z\in W\mid Q)$, $0.759$ by $\ln\mathrm{BF}$, $0.488$ against quasars just
outside the window, $0.988$ against stars and $0.964$ against galaxies, with the
clustering ratios $10.0$, $7.4$, $4.9$, $3.3$. It established several things this
note relies on:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The field model must contain every source type.} Its first validation
used a field fitted to point sources only and reported galaxies as a serious
contaminant (AUC $0.80$, $10.7\%$ above the same-redshift median); an all-source
field gave $0.96$ and $0.3\%$.
\item \emph{Training volume does not set the redshift precision.} Fifteen times
more quasars ($1{,}237{,}336$ against $79{,}865$) and twice the components moved
the median posterior width from $0.601$ to $0.602$.
\item \emph{A local field model hardly matters above $|b|=20^\circ$.} In $30$
cones from $|b|=20^\circ$ to $78^\circ$, a field model fitted in the
candidate's own cone described held-out field sources better by at most
$0.2$~nats per object, left the false-positive rate unchanged, and moved quasar
$\ln R$ by $0.1$.

\item \emph{The colour tails decide far from both models} (\S\ref{sec:outliers}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why it was retired.} It took one survey. A second model took any
combination of seven, but had no surface densities and so no $R$, and knew only
AllWISE, not the Legacy forced $W1,W2$; on identical $g,r,z$ the two were
equivalent, and the original's whole advantage came from those two bands. With
reference-band priors (\S\ref{sec:priors}) and the forced bands added, the
combined model matches or improves on the original on its own validation
(Table~\ref{tab:predecessor}), so there is no reason to keep two. The
$37$-band version of the combined model is archived beside it, with its priors
and its initial joint-only version.
